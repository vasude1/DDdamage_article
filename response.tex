\section*{Common response regarding Section 3.1}
The reviewers' comments indicate that Section 3.1 was insufficiently motivated in the submitted version. Accordingly, the section has been rewritten with an explicit statement of its purpose and scope.

Before a length scale is introduced, it must be established that any localization-band width observed in the regularized formulation of Sections 4,5 is a consequence of the regularization itself and not of the DDCM algorithm. The DDCM algorithm, when driven by a local database, must therefore reproduce the characteristic behaviors of local damage models - mesh dependence of the localization width, and softening-induced instabilities such as snapback. Both must be shown to be handled correctly by the DDCM algorithm before a regularized formulation is proposed. This is the purpose of Section 3.1, now stated explicitly at lines www--yyy of the unmarked manuscript.

A second concern is that the performance of the DDCM solver also depends on the resolution of the database, particularly under non-homogeneous strain increments within a load step. It is therefore necessary to determine whether a given pathology observed in a DDCM setting with a local model is a consequence of the algorithm itself or of insufficient database resolution. One way to isolate algorithmic effects from database sampling artifacts, and to make an analytical treatment tractable, is to replace the database by the underlying constitutive model. This is the motivation for using the model directly during the material update, now stated at lines www--xxx of the unmarked manuscript. On this basis, three cases are studied in Section 3.1 to analyze the effect of the hyperparameter and of the choice of solver (arc-length vs. direct displacement control): the hyperparameter choice under continuous evolution, the hyperparameter choice under snapback, and the continuation strategy under snapback. These three cases together establish the ingredients required for DDCM to inherit the correct behavior of a local damage model.

\section*{Other general modifications}

In addition to the items detailed below, the revision includes the following clarifications, made at our own initiative to support the arguments of Section~3.1 and the 2D examples:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A paragraph delimiting the range of validity of the spectral analysis has been added at the start of ``Failure with snapback and displacement control’’. It states that $\rho(\bfA\bfC)$ governs the AM iteration only while the material projection remains interior to the admissibility constraints of Definition~\ref{def:branch_restrictions}; when either constraint activates, the iteration leaves the regime governed by $\bfA\bfC$. This clarifies why the $C_2 = E$ snapback iterates do not diverge even though $\rho > 1$ -- the plateau is a constraint-saturation effect, not a divergence.
	\item The three cases of Section~3.1 are now summarized in Table~\ref{tab:necessity_summary}, which gathers the regime, hyper-parameter choices, and outcomes in one place.
	\item The behavior under arc-length control without snapback (case~\ref{case:rate} driven by arc length) is now treated explicitly: $C_2 > -E/N$ is required for convergence, so both $C_2 = m$ and $C_2 = E$ converge under arc length, with $C_2 = E$ approaching a spectral radius close to one. This closes the (snapback vs.\ no snapback) $\times$ (arc-length vs.\ displacement control) grid.
	\item Section~6.1 and the Discussion now acknowledge that the formulation does not include a tension-compression split of the strain energy, with references to \cite{amor2009regularized, miehe2010phase}. For the notched bar this is immaterial because the loading produces a purely tensile stress state along the crack path; the implementation of a tension-compression split in the data-driven setting is identified as an open problem in the Discussion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Reviewer 1}


In this paper, the authors adopt the framework of data-driven damage mechanics in which the solution to a boundary value problem is the minimization of a distance between strain and stress pairs with respect to a database subject to the stress-strain pairs satisfying equilibrium. Specifically for this paper, the authors consider the case of damage mechanics in which the stress-strain pairs in the database are history dependent and can exhibit softening. Authors show that the distance function must account for the softening in order to converge. Additionally, the paper tackles the problem of localization. Damage mechanics problems are known to localize to a single element. The regularization introduced is to restrict the norm of the strain to a certain interval. This Lipschitz regularization operates on the strain field because of the nature of the data-driven framework (which must work on the stress-strain field rather than on the elements of the material database), and introduces a length scale that prevents localization of the damage field. The paper is meritorious but some of the details are cumbersome to read and understand and the figure layout is also cumbersome.

\begin{equation*}
	(\epsst,\sigst) = \arg \min_{(\tilde{\epsst},\tilde{\sigst})} d(\bfeps,\bfsig,\tilde{\epsst},\tilde{\sigst}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tilde{\sigst}=f(\tilde{\epsst};\,\mathcal{H}),

We thank the reviewer for the comment. The motivation for substituting the database with an underlying model has now been presented in Section 3.1. As stated in the common response, the intent of this substitution is to analyze the convergence properties of the DDCM algorithm analytically in a simple 1D setting, which cannot be carried out directly with a finite database.

The case of the explicit model is a ``best case scenario'' check for DDCM: failure of the DDCM algorithm (for instance, to properly localize) with the substitution will necessarily imply its eventual failure with a finite database, since a finite database introduces further sources of error (sampling, limited coverage of the state space) that can only degrade the behavior observed here. The three cases examined in Section 3.1 therefore establish the ingredients necessary in the DDCM setting, and these conclusions are verified first in Section 3.2 (1D bar with body force, with branch restriction removed) and then in the 2D setting of Section 3.3.

The algorithmic criteria identified in this work are therefore expected to carry over to the finite-database setting; the only remaining source of discrepancy between the two settings is the database itself (sampling density, phase-space coverage), whose systematic study is left as future work.

\item Another crucial point concerns the first 1D example (page 8 and 9). In the test it is explained that the procedure converges at slow rate, however there is no common point between mechanical and material states (see figure 3a). What does this mean? It seems a proof of failure of the procedure: please explain.

\color{blue}
\textbf{Response.} We thank the reviewer for raising this point; it allows us to clarify what Section 3.1 is designed to establish and what Figure 3a is showing.

Section 3.1 performs a spectral analysis of the AM fixed-point map on an already-bifurcated branch. Its central deliverable is an analytical relation between the pair (hyper-parameter, continuation strategy) and the rate at which the mechanical and material states approach each other.

Figure 3a corresponds to the case ($C_2 = E$, displacement control, no snapback). For this case the analysis predicts that the composed update operator $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}$ has spectral radius $\rho \approx 0.9994$: the iteration is formally contractive, but at a rate indistinguishable from stagnation on the time scale of a load step. Figure 3a is the empirical confirmation of this prediction. The mechanical and material states would eventually meet, but only after a prohibitive number of iterations. This is therefore not a failure of the DDCM procedure, but a predicted diagnostic for a specific hyper-parameter choice. In fact, the figure exposes a practical pitfall: standard DDCM implementations declare convergence when successive iterates cease to move, and under $C_2 = E$ with the analytical database used here, the iterates become near-stationary long before the mechanical--material gap has closed. In a finite-database implementation, this behavior would be even more subtle: since $\rho \approx 0.9994$ implies a slow variation of the mechanical state between iterations, the nearest-neighbor material projection would select the same database point for many consecutive iterations, and the stopping criterion would then falsely declare convergence well before the distance functional was close to zero. This diagnosis is only visible when the exact spectral radius is known. To make this explicit, the normalized distance functional has been added in Figure~\ref{fig:dist_all_cases}, with a zoomed panel (Figure~\ref{dist_evol_1_zoom}) showing the residual decaying at a slope consistent with $\rho \approx 0.9994$.


The same spectral analysis delivers the complete picture across the (hyper-parameter, continuation strategy) combinations examined in
Section 3.1 and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:necessity_summary}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{No snapback, displacement control, $C_2 = E$}: 	$\rho \approx 0.9994$. Converges, unusably (Figure 3a).
	\item \emph{No snapback, displacement control, $C_2 = m$}: $\rho = 1/2$. 	Converges in a handful of iterations.
	\item \emph{Snapback, arc-length, $C_2 = E$}: $\rho \approx 1.029 > 1$. The iteration is non-contractive. It does not diverge, but freezes at a finite mechanical--material gap, because the material projection saturates at the peak stress  (constraint~\ref{br:peak_cap}) and the iteration leaves the regime governed by $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}$.
	\item \emph{Snapback, arc-length, $C_2 = m$}: $\rho = 1/2$. Converges in a handful of iterations.
\end{itemize}

Beyond these four cases, the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}$ in the arc-length setting yields a closed-form bound equation~\eqref{eq:c2_bound},  --- a derivation made possible by the substitution of the discrete database by the underlying analytical model: under snapback, the AM fixed-point map is contractive only when $C_2$ is strictly negative ($<-E/N$). No positive hyper-parameter is admissible, and $C_2 = m$ is the unique interpretable derivative of the constitutive response that lies in the admissible range. Figure 3a is therefore one entry in a complete analytical map, and the behavior it displays is the predicted signature of the only $C_2 > 0$ case that even converges in principle.

\item Section 4 - Why is the mesh adopted for the strain called "dual" with respect to the one used for displacements? The same order of interpolation is used for displacement and strain, does this ensure the fulfilment of the Babuska-Brezzi conditions? What are the consequences of these interpolations?

\color{blue}
\textbf{Response.} We thank the reviewer. Following the terminology of \cite{Chevaugeon2021}, the auxiliary mesh is now called the \emph{lip-mesh} throughout. The term ``dual'' was used only because its vertices are the centroids of the primal mesh; it carries no further meaning. A stability analysis of the displacement-strain pairing is provided in Appendix \ref{stab_check_lip_disp}: writing the compatibility constraint $\bfeps = \bfB \bfu$ in weak form, $\int \mu(\varepsilon - Bu)\, dx = 0$ with $\mu$ chosen as a sum of Diracs at the centroids of the primal mesh, recovers the discrete system in use. The resulting inf-sup constant then satisfies
\begin{equation*}
	\beta = \inf_{\bf \Lambda} \sup_{\bfU} \frac{b^h(\bfU, \bf \Lambda)}{\|\bfU\|\,\|\bf \Lambda\|} \geq 1
\end{equation*}
independently of the mesh, so the displacement-strain pairing satisfies the Babuska-Brezzi condition and no spurious modes arise from this interpolation choice. A separate inf-sup condition arises from the active-set multipliers that enforce the Lipschitz inequality; a formal analysis of this active-set formulation is beyond the scope of this article, and the stability of the discretized problem is instead assessed numerically through mesh refinement, as reported in Sections 4 and 5.

\item The removal of the constraint in order to reach failure (zero force), see fig 15, is artificial and put in evidence a non-physical behavior of the model. Also, in the L shape example failure cannot be reached. What is the reason of this?

\color{blue}
\textbf{Response.} We agree that the deactivation of the constraint at $d = 1$ is an artificial numerical device rather than a physical statement, and this is now stated plainly in Section 4.2. The deactivation is tractable in 1D because failure is contained in a single element and the discontinuity it introduces does not propagate; in 2D it causes convergence difficulties, and it has been replaced in Section 5 by a principled alternative: a damage-dependent weakening of the constraint combined with a log-barrier formulation. The 2D results reported in Sections 5 and 6 therefore rely on this smooth alternative, not on the hard deactivation.

Regarding why the L-beam simulation cannot be driven to complete failure: the L-beam develops a compressive region on the right side of the beam under the applied loading. The present formulation does not include a tension-compression split of the strain energy (see \cite{amor2009regularized, miehe2010phase}), so under purely compressive stress the model allows the damage variable to grow, which is unphysical. The simulation is therefore terminated at the load step where damage in the compressive zone begins to increase. This is now stated explicitly in Section~6.2. By contrast, the notched bar of Section 6.1 is a pure tension problem; no compressive region develops there, and the simulation can therefore be driven all the way to failure. The implementation of a tension-compression split in the data-driven setting is identified as an open problem in the Discussion.
